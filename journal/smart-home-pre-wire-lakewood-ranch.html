Smart home pre-wire for a Lakewood Ranch new build

Cable, boxes and conduit are cheap before drywall and expensive after it. Here is what to run in a Lakewood Ranch new build and when.

If you are building in Lakewood Ranch, the cheapest moment to decide how the house will be wired is the few weeks between framing and drywall. In Waterside, Lake Club, Star Farms, Azario and the newer villages, production and semi-custom builders move quickly, and the low-voltage stage often passes before the owner has thought about where the rack, the access points or the shade motors go. Once insulation and drywall are in, every one of those decisions turns into cutting, fishing and patching.

Here is what goes into a smart home pre-wire for a Lakewood Ranch new build, how it fits the builder's schedule, and what skipping it costs.

Why the pre-wire matters more in a Florida new build

Many new homes on both the Manatee and Sarasota sides of the Ranch are concrete block (CBS) on the ground floor, with wood-framed interior walls, a wood-framed second floor where there is one, and a truss roof. That mix decides where cable can go later and where it cannot.

Exterior block walls are the hard part. A cable or a box on an exterior wall either goes in the furring space before drywall or gets chased into the block afterward, which is slow, loud and visible. The attic is the other route, and in Southwest Florida it is a poor place to work: summer attic temperatures make retrofit pulls slow, and the trusses are tied down with hurricane straps and clips that nobody should be drilling through to save a few feet of cable. A house wired at rough-in avoids all of that.

There is also the lanai. Screen enclosures (the aluminum lanai cage) usually go up late in the build, often by a separate contractor. If there is no conduit stubbed out to the cage posts and the outdoor ceiling, outdoor speakers, cameras and lighting end up on surface-mounted wiremold or wireless gear that struggles with the heat and humidity.

What we run before drywall

A pre-wire is not a box of spare cable. It is a plan: every location marked on the drawings, every cable labelled at both ends, and every run going back to one place. On a typical Lakewood Ranch new build that plan covers:

  • Cat6A home runs to every TV location, office, media room and bedroom wall that may host a screen or a desk, each run returning to the rack rather than daisy-chained
  • Ceiling access point locations, one Cat6A drop each, placed from a coverage plan rather than one per room by default, including the bonus room, the primary suite and the lanai
  • Camera locations with a Cat6A drop for PoE at eaves, the garage, the front entry and the lanai, so cameras take power and data from the switch and need no outlet
  • Shade pockets or recesses at the windows where the builder will frame them, with low-voltage or line-voltage power at the head of each window depending on the shade system
  • Keypad locations in deep boxes at the main entries, the kitchen, the primary bedroom and wherever scenes will be used, so keypads stack cleanly with the electrician's switches
  • In-ceiling and in-wall speaker locations with speaker wire back to the rack, plus backboxes or brackets so the ceiling is cut once, in the right place
  • Conduit from the house to the lanai cage, pool equipment, a dock if the lot has water access, and a driveway gate or entry pillars, with pull string left in
  • A rack location in conditioned space with its own dedicated circuit, room for airflow, and conduit to the attic and to the service entrance for the internet provider

Two items deserve a word. Shades are where most retrofits go wrong: a motorized roller shade needs a pocket or a clean head detail and power at the window, and neither can be added cleanly after drywall and trim. If you are even considering motorized shades on the west and south elevations, wire for them now; the shading page covers how we size and pick the motors. And the rack belongs inside the air-conditioned envelope, typically an interior closet or a corner of the laundry room, not the garage and not the attic. Heat is what shortens the life of switches, recorders and power supplies in this climate.

Low-voltage pre-wire vs the builder's tech package

Most builders in the Ranch offer a structured wiring or technology package at the design center. Some are a handful of coax and Cat5e or Cat6 outlets and a small panel in a closet; others are more complete. Ask the same questions either way:

  • What cable is it: Cat6A, Cat6 or Cat5e, and are the runs home runs or looped between outlets?
  • Where does everything terminate, and is that location in conditioned space with power and room for a rack?
  • Are there any ceiling drops for access points, or any camera drops at the eaves?
  • Is there anything for shades, keypads or the lanai, or only data and TV outlets?
  • Who labels and tests the cables, and do you get a map of what went where?

A builder's package is not wrong; it is usually just written for a different house than the one you want. Often the right answer is to keep the builder's electrical scope as it is, decline or trim the tech package, and have a dedicated low-voltage contractor rough in to a plan drawn for the systems you actually intend to install.

Coordinating with the builder's schedule

Builders run on a sequence, and low-voltage has to fit inside it. First, we mark up the plan set before or at framing: device locations, box types, shade details, conduit and the rack. Those markups go to the builder and the electrician so nothing surprises them on site. Second, after framing and the electrical rough-in, and before insulation, we run the low-voltage rough-in, ideally in one visit,. Third, we walk the job with you and, if possible, the superintendent, confirming every location on the studs rather than on paper.

After drywall and paint, trim-out is when devices go in: keypads, speakers, access points, cameras and the rack itself. Some builders allow trim before closing, others only after; both work if the rough-in was done right. We work from your builder's schedule, not ours, and we do not promise dates that depend on someone else's calendar. More on how we work alongside builders and designers is on the designers and builders page.

One practical note for buyers: confirm early, in writing, that your builder allows an outside low-voltage contractor on site before closing. Policies differ by builder, and it is easier to settle at contract than after framing.

Retrofit later vs wire now

Skipping the pre-wire rarely saves money; it moves the cost later and makes it larger. In a finished Lakewood Ranch home, running a new cable to an exterior block wall means chasing or surface conduit. Pulling through a hot attic past straps, blocking and insulation takes far longer than the same run through open studs. Shades without pockets get mounted on the face of the window trim. Cameras without PoE drops end up on Wi-Fi with batteries or local power supplies. And the rack ends up where there happens to be space, which in many houses is the garage.

There is a middle path if the budget is tight at build time: run the cable and conduit now, and install the equipment later. Cable and boxes are a small part of a finished system and the only part that is truly hard to add afterward. Speakers, keypads, shades and cameras can all be installed after you move in, as long as the wire is waiting for them.

For a sense of scale, our budget estimator uses a range of about $1.60 to $3.40 per square foot of heated and cooled space for new-construction pre-wire and infrastructure, before any devices or control systems. It is an indicative range, not a quote; the real number depends on how many locations you want and what the house looks like on paper.

How to start before your framing date

The best time to call is when you have a plan set and a rough idea of the builder's schedule, ideally before framing. Bring the floor plan and a short list of what you want the house to do: shades on the west side, music on the lanai, cameras at the gate, Wi-Fi in the bonus room. We mark up the plans and tell you what has to happen before drywall and what can wait. Our Lakewood Ranch page covers the kinds of homes we see across the Ranch, and when you are ready, send us your plans.

Frequently asked questions

When should I schedule a smart home pre-wire in Lakewood Ranch?+

Before framing if you can, so locations are on the plans. The low-voltage rough-in itself happens after framing and the electrical rough-in, and before insulation and drywall. Once drywall is up, the pre-wire window has closed.

Is the builder's structured wiring package enough?+

Sometimes, but check what it includes: the cable type, whether runs are home runs, where they terminate, and whether there are any drops for access points, cameras, shades, keypads or the lanai. Many packages cover TV and data outlets only.

Why Cat6A instead of Cat6?+

Cat6A supports 10-gigabit Ethernet over full-length runs and handles PoE power for cameras and access points with less heat build-up in the bundle. The cable is a small share of the cost, and walls in a block house are expensive to reopen.

Can I pre-wire now and add equipment later?+

Yes. Running cable, conduit, deep boxes and shade power during construction, then installing speakers, cameras, keypads and shades after closing, is a common and sensible way to split the budget.

Where should the network rack go in a Florida new build?+

In conditioned space with a dedicated circuit and some airflow, usually an interior closet or part of the laundry room. Avoid the garage and the attic, where heat shortens equipment life.
