Smart home for snowbirds in Florida: running a seasonal house from up north

What to check when you arrive in the fall, what has to keep working while you are away, and how to close the house in April.

Late October is when the Gulf Coast fills back up. Cars with Ohio and Ontario plates reappear on Gulf of Mexico Drive, the lanai furniture comes out of the garage, and a house that sat closed for six months has to become a home again in a weekend. In April it all runs in reverse. For a seasonal owner, the smart home is not about voice control or party scenes. It is the set of systems that keeps an empty house dry, secure and reachable from a thousand miles away, and makes the two changeovers each year predictable.

This is how we think about a smart home for snowbirds in Florida: what to check when you arrive, what has to keep working while you are gone, and how to close the house in spring so that next October is uneventful.

Opening the house on arrival

The first hour in a seasonal house tells you how the summer went. Walk the systems in a fixed order, because each depends on the one before it.

  • Network first: check that the gateway, switch and Wi-Fi access points are online and that the internet line is the speed you pay for. Everything else rides on it.
  • Climate next: move the thermostats from the Away profile back to occupied setpoints, and look at the humidity history for the summer, not just today's reading.
  • Water: open the main if it was shut, run every tap and shower for a minute, and confirm each leak sensor still reports to the app.
  • Cameras and recorder: confirm the recorder kept writing all summer and check that the clock and storage are correct.
  • Shades and lighting: run each scene once. Motors that sat idle for months sometimes need their limits reset.
  • Locks and codes: retire the codes issued to summer contractors and confirm which ones your house manager still needs.

Expect firmware updates to have piled up. A controller, a set of access points and a camera recorder that were left alone for six months may all want updates at once. Run them in sequence during the first week, not the night before guests arrive, and note what changed.

Humidity and HVAC: the system that matters most while you are away

An empty Florida house does not usually fail through break-ins. It fails through moisture. A thermostat set too high to save power and a closed house can push indoor humidity well past 60 percent. Closets, mattresses and the backs of cabinets go first.

The practical setup is a connected thermostat, such as an Ecobee or a Honeywell Home model, running an Away profile with a cooling setpoint chosen for humidity rather than for the lowest bill, plus a dehumidify setting where the equipment supports it. Your HVAC contractor should set the exact numbers for your equipment. Then add independent temperature and humidity sensors in a closet and an interior room, reporting through the hub rather than through the thermostat itself. If the air handler fails, the thermostat that controls it may not tell you. A separate sensor will.

Set three alerts: humidity above your threshold for several hours, indoor temperature high enough that the AC is clearly not running, and the thermostat going offline. Three useful alerts beat twenty you learn to ignore.

Water, cameras, presence and access for vendors

Leak sensors are cheap insurance. Put them under the air handler and its condensate line, the water heater, the kitchen sink, the dishwasher, the ice maker and each toilet supply. A clogged condensate line is one of the most common summer failures in a closed Florida house, and a sensor in the drain pan catches it early. For a fuller answer, a whole-house shutoff valve such as a Moen Flo or a Phyn monitors flow at the main and can close the water automatically when it sees a leak, or when water runs while the house is supposed to be empty.

Cameras should earn their place. A recorder on site, such as UniFi Protect, keeps footage locally and lets you look in from your phone without paying a monthly fee to watch an empty driveway. Point cameras at the entry, the garage, the dock or seawall side and the pool equipment rather than at the street. Glare and foliage decide placement more than the floor plan does.

For presence, a Lutron system can run a randomized evening schedule in a few rooms, with landscape lighting on an astronomical clock. Keep it modest. A house lit like a dinner party every night reads as staged.

Most seasonal owners rely on a house watch service, a cleaner, a pool company and the occasional contractor. A smart lock from Yale or Schlage that connects to the home system lets you give each one their own code, limited to the days and hours they work, and see each entry in a log. When the pool company changes staff, you delete one code instead of rekeying the house. Pair the lock with a camera at the door, and an entry alert tells you both who came in and that it was actually them.

Gated communities add a layer. Many developments in Lakewood Ranch and Venice run their own gate access systems, and the HOA controls who gets a visitor pass. Your home system cannot open that gate, but it keeps a clean record of who entered your door and when.

Network reliability: everything else depends on it

Every alert in this article travels over the house network. If the modem locks up in July and nobody is there to unplug it, every camera and sensor goes silent and you will not know until something else goes wrong. A seasonal house needs a network built to recover by itself: a business-grade gateway such as UniFi, a UPS on the rack, managed power that can restart the modem remotely, and a cellular backup that takes over when the cable or fiber line drops. Closing the house in spring

The close-up is where a little discipline pays off all summer. Run the same written checklist every year, and test the alerts before leaving rather than after landing up north.

  • Switch thermostats to the Away profile and confirm the humidity setting is active
  • Test one leak sensor by touching it with a damp cloth and confirm the alert reaches your phone
  • Replace batteries in leak, humidity and door sensors, even if the app shows them as fine
  • Decide whether the water main stays open for irrigation or is shut by the automatic valve
  • Confirm the recorder has space for the summer and every camera is recording
  • Set the lighting presence schedule and turn off scenes nobody needs
  • Issue time-limited lock codes to the house watch, cleaner and pool service; delete the rest
  • Unplug the modem for a minute and check the house still reports over the cellular backup
  • Leave a written contact sheet for whoever has a key: who to call for HVAC, water and the network

Island, condo and HOA realities

Location changes the details. On Longboat Key and Siesta Key, salt air shortens the life of exterior hardware, so outdoor cameras and fixtures need marine-rated finishes, and many residences are condos where the association controls the exterior walls, the roof and the common network closet. In a condo you usually cannot drill the facade for a camera or run cable through common areas without board approval, so plans have to work inside the unit. Leak sensors and an automatic shutoff matter even more there, because a leak in your unit becomes your downstairs neighbor's ceiling.

In Lakewood Ranch and Venice, the typical seasonal home is a single-family house in a deed-restricted community. Architectural review boards often have rules on visible cameras, exterior lighting color and anything mounted to the roofline, so it is worth checking before work begins. Gated access is common, and many communities have their own house watch vendors who already know the neighborhood.

Frequently asked questions

What thermostat setting should I use when my Florida home is empty for the summer?+

Set it for humidity, not just temperature. Use dehumidification where the equipment supports it, and let your HVAC contractor set the exact numbers. Add a separate humidity sensor so you hear about a problem even if the thermostat does not.

Do I need a monthly subscription to watch my cameras from up north?+

Not with an on-site recorder. A system such as UniFi Protect stores footage in the house and lets you view it remotely from your phone. You need a reliable internet line, ideally with a cellular backup, but no paid cloud plan.

How do I give cleaners and contractors access without handing out keys?+

Use a connected smart lock with individual codes for each service, limited to their working days and hours. Every entry is logged, and a code can be deleted the day a vendor changes staff, without rekeying the house.

Can I add smart home equipment in a Longboat Key or Siesta Key condo?+

Usually yes, inside the unit. Thermostats, leak sensors, an automatic water shutoff, lighting control and interior networking rarely need board approval. Anything on the exterior walls, the roof or in common areas typically does, so check the association rules first.
